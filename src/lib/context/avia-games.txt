High-level summary:
CJ led a two-sprint consumer discovery engagement with AviaGames through Duke Product Lab, analyzing over 1,000 survey responses and conducting qualitative user interviews to identify growth opportunities within AviaGames' player base. The project began as an investigation into whether Avia could responsibly expand into the "brain training" market — and evolved into a more nuanced strategic reframe after a literature review and Lumosity case study analysis revealed the legal and evidentiary risks of making cognitive enhancement claims. The final output was a research-backed insights report with segmentation analysis, EDA findings, interview quotes, a SWOT analysis, and prioritized product recommendations delivered to AviaGames.

The original problem statement:
AviaGames partnered with Duke Product Lab after collecting anecdotal evidence from users suggesting their games — particularly Solitaire Clash — may improve cognitive functioning. The team was tasked with assessing the validity of these player perceptions and determining how to appropriately respond to them in product and marketing strategy.

The core research questions guiding the project:
1. Why do users feel that gameplay improves cognition?
2. What does improved cognition look like to users?
3. What aspects of users' lives feel improved after playing Avia's games?

The strategic pivot:
Early in the project, the team evaluated whether AviaGames could enter the clinical "brain training" category. After conducting a literature review of academic research on gaming and cognition, analyzing the Lumosity FTC lawsuit as a case study, and consulting with an expert in cognitive aging, the team concluded that:
- The scientific evidence linking gaming to improved cognition is promising but limited — most studies rely on small samples, short intervention periods, lack active control groups, and fail to demonstrate real-world transfer effects
- Making clinical or medical claims about cognitive enhancement requires large-scale randomized controlled trials, blinded assessments, and clear evidence of real-world transfer — requirements that far exceed the scope of a product lab engagement
- The 2016 FTC enforcement action against Lumosity (resulting in a ~$2 million settlement) established a clear legal precedent: marketing cognitive improvement claims without rigorous clinical evidence is deceptive advertising

The team pivoted the research question from "Can Avia enter brain training?" to "How might Avia responsibly capture value from cognitively motivated players?" — shifting focus from medical claims to user motivation and perceived value.

Lumosity case study (and why it mattered for this project):
In 2016, the FTC took enforcement action against Lumos Labs — maker of the brain-training app Lumosity — for deceptive advertising. Lumosity claimed its games could improve memory, attention, processing speed, protect against cognitive decline, reduce cognitive effects of PTSD, ADHD, stroke, and chemotherapy. The FTC found these claims lacked the required "competent and reliable scientific evidence." Lumos Labs paid $2 million in redress and is prohibited from making cognitive benefit claims without high-quality, randomized, controlled, blinded human clinical trials.

This case directly shaped the project's pivot: Avia should not market cognitive benefits without clinical backing, but can still design experiences and positioning that resonate with cognitively motivated users — without making medical claims.

Research methodology:
The team used a mixed-methods approach across two sprints of consumer discovery.

Sample selection:
A structured survey was designed and distributed to AviaGames players, generating over 1,000 responses. The survey was built to capture how users engage with gameplay and the perceived value they associate with it — particularly around cognitive engagement (memory, focus, mental stimulation). The survey also included an opt-in component for follow-up interviews.

Quantitative analysis:
The team conducted exploratory data analysis (EDA) on survey responses to identify patterns across demographics and player motivations. This included analyzing response distributions and relationships between key variables.

Qualitative analysis:
Opt-in user interviews explored how players describe their experiences and the value they derive from gameplay, revealing nuances not captured by survey data alone.

Key EDA insights:

Insight 1 — Player base skews older and predominantly female:
Survey data shows approximately 70% female users and strong representation in middle-aged and older demographics. This aligns with patterns in casual, routine-based gaming, where users value relaxation, consistency, and low-friction engagement.

Insight 2 — Geographic distribution is broad:
Players are distributed across the U.S. with no single dominant region, suggesting observed behaviors and motivations are broadly representative rather than localized.

Insight 3 — Entertainment is the primary driver — but not the only one:
Most users play for fun and relaxation. However, a meaningful portion also associate gameplay with cognitive benefits. This suggests users layer additional meaning onto their experience beyond pure entertainment.

Insight 4 — Cognitive motivation exists as a secondary layer of value:
Cognitive engagement is often not the primary reason users begin playing, but becomes a reinforcing factor over time. Players interpret gameplay as mentally stimulating, which enhances perceived value and may support continued engagement.

Insight 5 — Cognitive perceptions are non-clinical and self-defined:
Most users who associate gameplay with cognitive benefits are not engaging with medical providers. These perceptions are personal and experiential, not grounded in clinical validation. This distinction is critical for responsible positioning.

Insight 6 — Opportunity lies in perception, not medical positioning:
The opportunity is not to enter the clinical brain training space, but to leverage user perceptions of cognitive engagement through product experience and positioning — without making medical claims.

User segmentation:
The team segmented users by primary motivation rather than demographics — a more actionable framework for product strategy.

Segment 1 — Entertainment-Driven Players (33.7% of respondents):
These users primarily engage with AviaGames for fun, relaxation, and enjoyment. Gameplay is a form of leisure. This segment represents the core user base and aligns closely with Avia's current product positioning. They derive value from low-friction, casual play.

Segment 2 — Cognitively Motivated Players (30.8% of respondents):
These users associate gameplay with mental stimulation — beliefs that playing may support memory, focus, or overall brain engagement. Critically, this segment does not view Avia as a medical or clinical tool; rather, they experience gameplay as something that feels mentally engaging. Their perceptions are self-defined, not clinically grounded.

Key insight about segment overlap:
These two segments are not mutually exclusive. Many entertainment-driven users also report experiencing cognitive engagement as a secondary benefit. This overlap is the most important strategic finding: the product opportunity lies not in building separate experiences for each segment, but in developing a shared value proposition that enhances both entertainment and perceived cognitive engagement simultaneously.

User interview findings:
Three recurring themes emerged from qualitative interviews with players across both segments:

Theme 1 — Engagement is interpreted differently across users:
Users describe nearly identical gameplay moments — but frame them as either relaxation or productivity depending on their motivation. Representative quotes: "While playing, I feel relaxed and happy… I don't think of anything except the game." and "It turns gameplay into something that feels productive, not just entertaining."

Theme 2 — Cognitive value is felt through experience, not clinical improvement:
Even cognitively motivated users do not associate gameplay with measurable cognitive gains. They describe moment-to-moment mental engagement, not lasting improvement. Representative quote: "I wouldn't say it improved my memory — it's more about figuring things out and adapting quickly."

Theme 3 — Strategy and problem-solving drive perceived value:
Players across segments value opportunities for planning, pattern recognition, and strategic thinking — which reinforce the perception of "using their brain." Representative quote: "The game rewards looking for second-order consequences… I've caught myself applying that same thinking to planning my workday."

Conclusions and strategic recommendations:

Core conclusion:
Across both segments, players engage with the game in nearly identical ways and derive value from the same moments of strategy, problem-solving, and engagement. Users do not view the product as a tool for measurable cognitive improvement — they view it as an experience that feels mentally stimulating. The current gameplay experience already delivers perceived cognitive value, not through clinical outcomes, but through engagement itself. Introducing heavier "brain training" mechanics or structured clinical positioning risks disrupting the casual, low-friction nature of play that drives engagement across both segments.

The opportunity is to make this existing value more visible — especially to cognitively motivated users — without changing the core product or making medical claims.

Recommendation 1 — Surface player progress and improvement [HIGH PRIORITY]:
Introduce badges, tiers, or achievement systems that reflect skill, consistency, and growth over time. This aligns with users' desire to "feel" improvement without changing the underlying gameplay. Makes perceived cognitive value tangible.

Recommendation 2 — Add lightweight performance feedback [HIGH PRIORITY]:
Highlight trends like streaks, personal bests, or improvement over time within the product experience. Makes cognitive value more tangible without formal cognitive evaluation or clinical framing.

Recommendation 3 — Preserve low-friction, casual gameplay [MEDIUM PRIORITY]:
Ensure any new features integrate seamlessly into short, repeatable sessions. Avoid added complexity or mechanics that feel evaluative or test-like, which would disrupt the casual experience that drives engagement across both user segments.

Recommendation 4 — Frame value as engagement, not medical benefit [HIGH PRIORITY]:
Position the experience as mentally stimulating rather than making explicit claims about memory or cognitive improvement. This directly mitigates FTC / Lumosity-type regulatory risk while still resonating with cognitively motivated players.
