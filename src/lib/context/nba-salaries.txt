- Project type: Sports analytics research, exploratory data analysis, multi-source data integration
- Collaborators: Athena Wemmert, Daryn Pham, Ian Couture, Nathan Levine

High-level summary:
CJ conducted a large-scale sports analytics project analyzing how NBA teams allocate salary and how those spending strategies relate to regular season success, playoff performance, and organizational efficiency. The project combined multi-source web data collection, data cleaning and transformation, dataset merging and normalization, exploratory data analysis, statistical visualization, custom metric construction, and research synthesis across NBA seasons from 2015–16 through 2025–26.

The broader goal was not simply to ask whether spending more leads to winning more, but to understand HOW teams spend, how salary concentration impacts outcomes, and what trends emerge across modern roster construction strategies.

Core project narrative:
Most people assume higher payroll automatically leads to winning championships. This project explored a more nuanced question: what kinds of spending strategies actually create sustainable competitive success? The analysis examined whether expensive teams consistently outperform cheaper teams, whether superstar-heavy payroll structures outperform balanced rosters, whether championship teams follow predictable salary allocation patterns, how modern positional value has shifted across the NBA, and which organizations generate the best value relative to spending. The project is fundamentally about organizational strategy and efficiency, not just basketball statistics.

Research questions:
- Do higher payroll teams win more games?
- Are expensive teams more successful in the playoffs?
- How do teams allocate salary across positions and players?
- Is superstar-heavy spending more effective than balanced roster construction?
- Which teams generate the best value relative to spending?
- What salary and roster-building trends have emerged over the last decade?

Technical workflow:

Step 1 — Data collection:
The project pulled salary and performance data from HoopsHype, ESPN, and Basketball Reference using programmatic web table extraction through pandas and HTML parsing. Challenges included inconsistent naming conventions, varying salary formats, changing team abbreviations, midseason traded players appearing multiple times, and differently structured historical datasets.

Step 2 — Data cleaning and transformation:
Major transformations included removing formatting inconsistencies, standardizing team names and season formats, handling missing values, converting salary fields into numeric values, merging player and team datasets, and building derived metrics. The hardest part was ensuring datasets from different sources aligned correctly across teams, seasons, and players.

Step 3 — Exploratory analysis:
Key analyses included payroll vs. regular season wins, payroll vs. playoff advancement, cost-per-win comparisons, salary concentration analysis, positional spending trends, and superstar-heavy vs. balanced roster comparisons.

Step 4 — Custom metrics:

TopHeavyIndex: Measured how concentrated a team's payroll was among its top three highest-paid players. Helped evaluate whether teams relying heavily on star players performed differently from teams with more balanced salary distributions.

Cost Per Win: Calculated as Team Payroll divided by Regular Season Wins. Measured organizational efficiency rather than raw success.

Key findings:

Finding 1 — Higher payroll teams generally win more regular season games. The analysis found a positive relationship between payroll and regular season success. The project does not claim that spending guarantees championships.

Finding 2 — The highest payroll teams rarely win championships. Many championship teams did not have the league's highest payroll. Smart roster construction often matters more than pure spending power.

Finding 3 — Superstar-heavy teams often perform well. Teams concentrating salary among elite players frequently won more games, generated lower cost-per-win values, and performed efficiently. However, pure top-heaviness alone was not always optimal.

Finding 4 — Championship teams often balance stars with depth. Many successful teams fell into a middle range of salary concentration. The strongest organizations balance elite talent with functional depth and flexibility.

Finding 5 — Positional salary allocation has shifted over time. The project identified increasing investment in guards, playmakers, and versatile perimeter players, reflecting broader changes in modern NBA strategy.

Technologies used:
Python, pandas, NumPy, seaborn, matplotlib, Jupyter Notebook

Project challenges:
The biggest technical challenge was aligning inconsistent datasets across multiple public sources — different team abbreviations, midseason trades, salary formatting inconsistencies, missing records, and season formatting differences. The biggest analytical challenge was avoiding simplistic conclusions. A correlation between payroll and wins does not prove spending efficiency, and championship success is influenced by many contextual variables.
